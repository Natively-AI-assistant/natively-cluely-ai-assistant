## Lecture 3 (Wednesday 16 September 2026): Elasticity

### The house method

Every percentage change in an elasticity calculation in this course uses the midpoint method: the change divided by the average of the old and new values. I insist on it for one reason. With the ordinary percentage change you get one elasticity for a price rise and a different one for the same price cut. The midpoint method gives the same number in both directions.

For price elasticity of demand we report a positive number. Demand curves slope down, everyone knows the sign is negative, and carrying it around causes mistakes when you compare with 1. Drop it. For cross-price and income elasticity the sign carries the information, so keep it.

Demand is elastic when the figure is above 1, inelastic when it is below 1, and unit elastic at exactly 1.

### Worked example 1: the flat white at Brindle & Oat

The campus cafe raised the price of a flat white from 3.00 to 3.60 crowns. Daily sales fell from 440 cups to 360.

- Quantity: a fall of 80 on an average of 400, so 20%.
- Price: a rise of 0.60 on an average of 3.30, so 18.2%.
- Elasticity: 1.10. Demand is elastic.

Check with revenue: 1,320 crowns a day before the change, 1,296 after. Revenue fell when the price rose, which is what elastic demand looks like.

### Worked example 2: the monthly bus pass

The bus company raised the monthly pass from 38 to 42 crowns, and sales fell from 1,050 passes to 950.

- Quantity: down 100 on an average of 1,000, so 10%.
- Price: up 4 on an average of 40, so 10%.
- Elasticity: 1.00, unit elastic. Revenue is 39,900 crowns before and after.

I chose this one so that you see a price rise that leaves revenue exactly where it was.

### What makes demand more or less elastic: SPLAT

- S is for substitutes. The more close substitutes, the more elastic.
- P is for proportion of income. The bigger the share of the budget, the more elastic.
- L is for luxury or necessity. Luxuries are more elastic.
- A is for addictive or habitual. Habits make demand less elastic.
- T is for time. Given longer to adjust, demand becomes more elastic.

### The other elasticities, with the lecture's numbers

- Cross-price: oat milk went up 10% and flat white sales fell 4%. Cross-price elasticity is -0.4. Negative means complements.
- Income: student stipends rose 10% and instant noodle sales fell 3%. Income elasticity is -0.3. Negative means an inferior good.

### Who carries a tax

The less elastic side of the market carries more of a tax. Lecture example: a tax of 0.50 crowns per cup. The price buyers pay rises by 0.30 and the price sellers keep falls by 0.20, so buyers carry 60% of it. That tells you demand for coffee near campus is less elastic than supply.

### Something I said too loosely

"Elasticity is just the slope." It is not, and I should not have said it. Along a straight-line demand curve the slope is the same everywhere, but elasticity is above 1 at high prices and below 1 at low prices. Slope uses changes in units; elasticity uses percentage changes.

## Follow-ups to lecture 5 (inflation and monetary policy), added 1 October

**"Behind the curve."** By this I mean one specific thing: the Board's actual policy rate is below the rate that the classroom rule prescribes for today's inflation and output gap. The distance between the two is how far behind it is.

**"Real rates are negative right now."** I was talking about the policy rate measured in real terms, that is, the policy rate less current inflation. I was not talking about the savings account on the same slide, which is a separate example.

**"Always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon."** That is Friedman's line, and I put it on the slide to provoke you. The position we take in ECN 1620: over the long run, sustained inflation needs money growth to feed it; in the short run it can start from demand or from costs.

**The 6 quarters.** A rule of thumb for Norvale, not a law of nature. Larger and more closed economies are usually slower.
